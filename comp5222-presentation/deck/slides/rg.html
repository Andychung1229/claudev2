Biology background

We summarise the ensemble by its size: radius of gyration, Rg

What Rg measures

The root-mean-square distance of residues from the chain’s centre of mass, averaged over the ensemble. Compact chain → small Rg; expanded chain → large Rg. Measured in the lab by SAXS.

Length sets the baseline

Polymer physics: Rg ≈ R0 · N^ν. Fitted on our 18,860 training chains:

ν = 0.53

Sequence decides the rest

At the same length, chains differ because pairs of residues attract or repel:

  • Charges and how they are patterned (electrostatics)
  • Aromatic “stickers” such as W, Y, F
  • Prolines and other residues that stiffen or expand the chain

Same length, different sequence → different Rg.

[~1:00] Since there is no single structure, we need a number that summarises the ensemble. The standard one is the radius of gyration, Rg: how far, on average, the residues sit from the centre of mass. A compact chain has a small Rg, an expanded chain a large one. It can be measured experimentally with small-angle X-ray scattering, SAXS, which is why it is the main benchmark for sequence-to-ensemble predictors. Two things set Rg. First, length. Polymer physics says Rg grows like N to the power nu. When we fit our training data we get nu around 0.53 — between an ideal chain at 0.5 and a fully expanded chain at about 0.59. Second, and more interesting, the sequence. Two chains of the same length can have quite different sizes, because pairs of residues attract or repel each other: charges and their patterning, aromatic “sticker” residues like tryptophan and tyrosine, and prolines. The key word here is pairs. That is what motivates a graph.
