[~1:15] Step two: turning a sequence into a graph. This is a real thirty-six-residue sequence from our test set. Every residue becomes a node. The node feature is just a one-hot code for the amino acid plus its relative position along the chain. No large pretrained embeddings. Then we add two kinds of edges. Local edges, in blue, connect each residue to its neighbours up to two positions away, plus a self-loop. They keep the graph connected, like the backbone. Non-local edges, in orange, are where the contact prior comes in. For every residue we score all residues more than two positions away: the contact frequency of that amino-acid pair, divided by the average contact frequency at that sequence distance. Each residue keeps its eight highest-scoring partners, and we make the edges undirected. Here you can see the choice for one residue, phenylalanine sixteen: it connects to the cysteines, phenylalanines and other residues further down the chain. These edges are candidate interactions, not measured contacts: they come only from the sequence and the frozen table.
